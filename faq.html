Study Basics

What is this study about?

This is a 94+ day continuous monitoring study of a DIY 12V 500Ah LiFePO₄ battery bank. The primary goal is to validate "architectural immunity" — the principle that parallel-connected mixed-brand cells behave as a monolithic unit through topology rather than requiring cell-level matching.

Secondary objectives include quantifying long-term voltage drift, assessing storage viability, and demonstrating rigorous DIY battery monitoring methodology.

Why does this matter?

Many DIY battery builders worry that mixing cell brands or ages will cause imbalance and premature failure. This study provides evidence that properly configured parallel cells naturally share current and maintain common bus potential, regardless of manufacturer or internal resistance variations.

Practical impact: Builders can confidently use cost-effective mixed-brand cells rather than expensive matched sets, as long as basic quality standards (Grade A cells) and proper electrical connections are maintained.
What does "architectural immunity" actually mean?

It means the parallel battery topology itself enforces voltage equality — cells must share the same bus potential because they're electrically connected. Current distributes naturally based on internal resistance, but the voltage remains common.

This is "immunity" because it doesn't require active management, cell matching, or special electronics. The architecture handles it passively through Kirchhoff's voltage law.

Is this peer-reviewed research?

No, this is independent research published through GitHub and Zenodo DOI. It has not undergone formal peer review. However, it follows open science principles:

  • Complete raw data publicly available
  • Methodology fully documented
  • Analysis code provided for verification
  • Limitations and uncertainties explicitly stated

Readers are encouraged to scrutinize the methods and data independently.

Architectural Immunity

Can I really mix different battery brands safely?

For parallel connections: Yes, with important caveats:

  • ✅ All cells must be the same chemistry (all LiFePO₄, not mixing with LiPo or NMC)
  • ✅ Use Grade A cells (avoid mixing with Grade B/C cells)
  • ✅ Ensure proper bus connections with low resistance
  • ✅ Always use a BMS for protection (architectural immunity doesn't replace safety)
  • ⚠️ Cells should have similar capacity ratings (don't mix 100Ah with 300Ah)

For series connections: Mixing brands in series is riskier and not recommended. Series cells must have matched capacity.

What about cells with different internal resistance?

Cells with different internal resistance naturally share current proportionally. Lower-resistance cells carry slightly more current, but this is not a problem because:

  • They still maintain common bus voltage
  • Current distribution is stable and doesn't cause divergence
  • Higher-resistance cells aren't "overloaded" — they simply contribute less current

As long as resistance differences aren't extreme (e.g., one cell with 10× the resistance of others), the system remains stable.

Does architectural immunity mean I don't need a BMS?
NO. Always use a BMS.

Architectural immunity addresses voltage balance in parallel configurations, not protection from:

  • Over-voltage (can damage cells)
  • Under-voltage (permanent capacity loss)
  • Over-current (fire/explosion risk)
  • Temperature extremes (thermal runaway)

A BMS is essential safety equipment regardless of cell configuration.

Haven't people reported problems mixing battery brands?

Yes, but most reported issues stem from:

  1. Poor connections: High-resistance bus connections cause voltage drops that look like divergence
  2. Grade B/C cells: Low-quality cells with high self-discharge or defects
  3. Series configurations: Mixing in series requires capacity matching (different issue)
  4. Measurement artifacts: Voltage readings taken at cell terminals instead of common bus

With proper construction and Grade A cells, parallel mixing is well-supported by electrical principles.

Methodology & Measurements

Why measure bus voltage instead of individual cell voltages?

Two reasons:

  1. Testing architectural immunity: The hypothesis is that cells share common bus potential. Measuring the bus directly tests this claim.
  2. Practical monitoring: Most DIY builders use bus-level monitoring (shunts, BMS) rather than per-cell sensing. This study reflects real-world usage.

Limitation acknowledged: Cannot detect individual cell divergence that might exist below the measurement resolution. Future work could add per-cell monitoring for comparison.

What is "spread" and why does it increase during Eco Mode?

In this study, "spread" is the hourly max−min voltage from a single bus-level sensor. It does NOT measure cell-to-cell voltage differences.

Eco Mode increase: When the SmartShunt entered power-save mode, sampling became sparse (intermittent instead of continuous 10s intervals). This sparse sampling causes aliasing — the sensor misses intermediate voltage values and only catches peaks/troughs, making spread appear larger.

This is a measurement artifact, not evidence of battery divergence. The voltage itself didn't become more variable; we just sampled it less frequently.
Why not use temperature data from inside the battery?

Battery core temperature sensors weren't installed in this configuration. Ambient temperature was used because:

  • Battery was stored indoors in climate-controlled space (minimal thermal gradient)
  • Goal was to quantify observable thermal correlation, not precise cell thermodynamics
  • System-level coefficient includes both cell chemistry and measurement chain effects

Future studies could add thermocouples to cells for more precise thermal analysis.

How confident are you in the drift rate measurements?

Very confident for the measured period. Drift analysis shows:

  • R² > 0.95 for linear regression (excellent fit)
  • Consistent trend over 70+ days
  • Clear deceleration in late period (drift approaching equilibrium)

Uncertainty: ±0.1 mV/day due to regression fit and sampling noise. The −0.67 mV/day (stasis) and −0.17 mV/day (late) values are robust.

Caveat: Long-term projection (7-10 months to 80% SOC) assumes drift continues at late-period rate. Actual performance may vary with temperature, self-discharge chemistry changes, etc.

Safety

Are lithium batteries dangerous?
Yes, lithium batteries carry inherent risks. A 500Ah bank stores ~6 kWh of energy and can deliver thousands of amps in a short circuit.

LiFePO₄ (LFP) chemistry is much safer than other lithium types (NMC, NCA, LiPo) because:

  • Non-flammable chemistry (iron phosphate vs cobalt/nickel)
  • Stable at high temperatures
  • Lower energy density = less violent failures

However, safety precautions are still essential.

What safety equipment is required?

Mandatory:

  • ✅ BMS — Over/under voltage, over-current, temperature protection
  • ✅ Class T fuses at battery terminals (properly rated for DC voltage and current)
  • ✅ Class D fire extinguisher accessible (for metal fires)
  • ✅ Smoke detector near battery location

Recommended:

  • Temperature monitoring with alerts
  • Fireproof/fire-resistant enclosure or metal battery box
  • Adequate ventilation (hydrogen gas risk during failure)
  • Circuit breaker or disconnect switch
Can I charge LiFePO₄ batteries in freezing temperatures?
NO. Never charge below 0°C (32°F).

Charging lithium batteries below freezing causes lithium plating on the anode, which:

  • Permanently reduces capacity
  • Increases internal resistance
  • Can create internal short circuits (dendrites)
  • Increases fire/failure risk

Discharging at low temperatures is safe (though capacity is reduced), but charging must wait until cells warm above 0°C.

What should I do if I smell something burning or see smoke?
Emergency Response:
  1. Disconnect power immediately (circuit breaker/fuse)
  2. Evacuate the area
  3. Call fire department — tell them it's a lithium battery fire
  4. Do NOT use water (use Class D extinguisher if fire is small)
  5. Do NOT attempt to move the battery

Lithium battery fires burn very hot and can reignite hours later. Professional response is essential.

Replication

Can I replicate this study with a smaller battery?

Yes! The principles apply at any scale. You can conduct a similar study with:

  • Any LiFePO₄ bank ≥100 Ah (larger is better for stability)
  • Voltage monitoring with ≤10 mV resolution
  • Data logging at ≤1 minute intervals
  • Minimum 30-day monitoring period

See the Replication Guide for detailed setup instructions.

What equipment do I need to replicate this?

Minimum setup:

  • LiFePO₄ battery bank with BMS
  • Victron SmartShunt or equivalent voltage monitor (10 mV resolution or better)
  • Data logging system (Home Assistant, Arduino, Raspberry Pi, etc.)
  • Temperature sensor (optional but recommended)

Estimated cost: ~$50-150 for monitoring equipment (excluding the battery itself).

Do I need Home Assistant specifically?

No. Any data logging system works as long as it can:

  • Collect voltage readings every 10-60 seconds
  • Store data in time-series format (CSV, database, etc.)
  • Maintain >99% uptime for the monitoring period

Alternatives:

  • Raspberry Pi + Python script + SQLite
  • Arduino/ESP32 with SD card logging
  • Commercial data logger
  • Node-RED + InfluxDB
What if my results don't match yours?

Investigate potential causes:

  • Cell quality: Grade B/C cells may show higher divergence
  • Connection resistance: Poor bus connections cause voltage drops
  • BMS balancing: Some BMS actively balance at rest, affecting voltage
  • Temperature swings: Large daily temperature variations increase drift variability
  • Measurement artifacts: Check sampling intervals and sensor accuracy

Document your findings and consider opening a GitHub issue to discuss. Divergent results are scientifically valuable!

Results & Interpretation

What does "99% capacity validation" mean?

The battery delivered 397 Ah in a controlled discharge test. This is 99.3% of the expected capacity when accounting for actual cell specifications (many "500 Ah" cells are slightly over-spec when brand new).

This validates that the mixed-brand parallel configuration doesn't suffer from capacity loss or imbalance issues.

Is 7-10 months storage realistic?

It's a conservative projection based on late-period drift rate (−0.17 mV/day). Real-world factors that could affect this:

  • Temperature: Higher temps increase self-discharge
  • BMS standby draw: Most BMS consume 20-50 mA continuously
  • Drift deceleration: Rate may slow further as battery approaches equilibrium

Bottom line: Battery should easily handle 6+ months of storage without attention. Projections beyond that are speculative.

Why did drift rate decrease over time?

Battery chemistry approaches electrochemical equilibrium at a given SOC. Early rapid drift represents:

  • Post-charge voltage relaxation
  • Initial self-discharge from higher energy state
  • Surface charge dissipation

As voltage drops and chemistry stabilizes, drift naturally slows. This is expected behavior and supports long-term storage viability.

What is the temperature coefficient useful for?

Knowing that voltage changes ~1 mV/°F helps interpret voltage readings:

  • Daily voltage variations often correlate with ambient temperature, not SOC changes
  • Comparing voltages requires temperature correction
  • SOC estimates from voltage are more accurate when temperature-compensated

Example: A 10°F temperature drop causes ~10 mV voltage decrease, which might be mistaken for self-discharge without this context.

Limitations & Future Work

What are the main limitations of this study?

Measurement scope:

  • Bus-level sensing only (cannot detect cell-level divergence below resolution)
  • Ambient temperature only (not battery core temperature)
  • Single battery configuration (N=1)

Operating conditions:

  • Stasis study (not cycling performance)
  • Indoor climate-controlled environment
  • Specific SOC range (~80-85%)

Generalizability:

  • Results may not apply to all mixed-brand configurations
  • Grade B/C cells not tested
  • Extreme temperature conditions not studied
What would make this study stronger?

Enhanced instrumentation:

  • Per-cell voltage monitoring for comparison with bus measurement
  • Battery core temperature sensors
  • Direct current measurement (instead of inferred from drift)

Expanded scope:

  • Multiple parallel banks to test reproducibility (N>1)
  • Cycling performance (charge/discharge cycles, not just stasis)
  • Temperature chamber testing (controlled thermal conditions)
  • Deliberate mixing of cell ages and grades

Longer duration:

  • Extend to 6-12 months to validate storage projections
  • Multiple seasonal cycles
Are you planning follow-up studies?

Potential future work includes:

  • Adding per-cell voltage monitoring for direct comparison
  • Cycling study (charge/discharge performance over many cycles)
  • Direct current measurement validation
  • Extended monitoring to validate 6+ month storage claims

Updates will be posted to the GitHub repository and Substack. Community contributions and replications are encouraged!

Can I contribute data from my own battery bank?

Yes! If you're conducting similar monitoring, consider:

  • Opening a GitHub discussion to share your setup and findings

Replication studies with different configurations, climates, and use cases would greatly strengthen the evidence base for architectural immunity.
